Many of us grew up between two worlds. At home it was Bangla, rice at dinner and relatives on
WhatsApp video calls. Outside it was school, work and friends who dated freely. When marriage comes
up, those two worlds meet at the dinner table, and the conversation can feel harder than it should.

This guide is for Bangladeshi diaspora singles in Canada, the UK, the US and beyond who want to
talk to their parents about marriage honestly, without a fight.

## Why this conversation feels so hard

Your parents are not just asking about a wedding. For many Bangladeshi families, a child's marriage
is tied to family reputation, faith, community and their own sense of having done their job well.
Many parents also carry worries they rarely say out loud: that you will marry someone who doesn't
share your values, that you will drift away from the family, or that time is running out.

You, meanwhile, may want a say in who you marry, a partner who understands life abroad, and a pace
that feels right. Both sides want a good marriage. You disagree on the route, not the destination.

## Start before there is a proposal on the table

The worst time to discuss what you want is when an aunty has already sent a biodata. Pick a calm
moment, with no guests and no pressure, and open with something like:

> "Ammu, Abbu, I want to get married, and I want you involved. Can we talk about how we go about it
> together?"

Leading with "I want you involved" lowers the temperature. It tells them you are not shutting them
out.

## Say what matters to you, specifically

Vague answers ("I'll know when I meet them") make parents more anxious. Be concrete:

- Values: faith practice, family closeness, how you want to raise children
- Life abroad: whether a partner from Bangladesh would need to relocate, and whether you would ever
  move back
- Education and work: what you need in a partner, and what you don't care about
- Timeline: a realistic window, such as "within the next two years"

Writing these down first helps. Some families find it easier to discuss a short list than a long
conversation.

## Ask what matters to them, and listen

Ask your parents the same questions: "What would make you feel comfortable about someone?" You may
find their must-haves are fewer than you feared, perhaps a good family, a practising Muslim or a
shared language, and that the rest is negotiable. Repeat back what you heard. Feeling understood
matters to parents as much as it does to you.

## Agree on how matches will be found

Many families now combine approaches: introductions through relatives and a trusted ghotok,
community events, and online matrimony platforms. Agree on the rules together:

- Who can suggest matches, and how you will be told
- When you will meet someone, and whether family will be present at first
- How quickly you will give an answer, so no one is left waiting

If you use apps or websites, tell your parents which ones and why. Showing them how
[privacy and verification](/blog/online-matrimony-safety-red-flags) work often eases their fear of
"strangers on the internet".

## When you disagree about a specific person

Disagreement is normal. Keep it about compatibility, not about winning:

- Ask for their specific concern, then address that concern only
- Suggest a family meeting or a video call with their family before any decision
- Ask a respected elder, khala or chacha whom both sides trust to give a view

If a match is truly important to you, stay respectful and patient. Pressure on either side rarely
ends well.

## If your parents are in Bangladesh

Time zones and distance make this harder. Schedule regular video calls instead of rushed messages,
share profiles and photos together on screen, and involve a trusted relative in your city who can
meet people in person.

## The goal: a decision you all can stand behind

The best outcome is not you winning or your parents winning. It is a marriage that you chose, with
a family that feels part of it. That starts with one honest conversation, and it usually goes
better than you expect.
