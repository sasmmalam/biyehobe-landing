In many cultures, marriage joins two families, not just two people. That is one of its great
strengths. It can also be a source of tension, when parents, siblings or relatives have strong
opinions about how a new couple should live.

Boundaries are not about pushing family away. They are about protecting your marriage so that
family relationships can stay warm for the long term.

## Start with each other

The most important rule: **the two of you agree first, then speak to family together.** A
boundary that one spouse sets alone, while the other quietly disagrees, rarely holds.

Talk through:

- What feels fine, and what feels like too much?
- Where do your families' expectations differ?
- What does each of you need to feel respected?

## Each partner speaks to their own family

A simple, effective rule many couples use: **you handle your parents, I'll handle mine.** A
message from a son or daughter lands very differently from the same message from a daughter- or
son-in-law. It also protects the in-law relationship from becoming the "bad guy". Our guide to
[talking to Bangladeshi parents about marriage](/blog/talking-to-bangladeshi-parents-about-marriage)
has tips.

## Common situations, and what to say

### Unannounced visits

> "We love having you here, Ammu. Could you call us before coming over? Some days we're both
> exhausted after work and we want to give you our full attention."

### Advice on parenting, cooking or the home

> "Thank you, I'll think about that." (Said kindly, without arguing, and without promising.)

Not every comment needs a debate. Pick the issues that really matter.

### Pressure about children

> "We're talking about it as a couple, and we'll share news when we're ready."

### Money requests

Agree as a couple on how you handle family financial support, then share that plan calmly. Our
guide to [talking about money before marriage](/blog/money-talk-before-marriage) covers this.

## Living in a joint family, or close by

Living with parents, or near them, is common and can be wonderful: shared meals, help with
children, grandparents who are part of daily life. It works best when:

- Everyone has some private space and time.
- Household responsibilities are clear.
- The couple can make decisions about their own life and children.
- There is a plan for what happens if it stops working.

If you are not yet married, discuss living arrangements before the wedding. It is one of the most
important [questions to ask before marriage](/blog/questions-to-ask-before-marriage).

## When an in-law is critical of your spouse

This is where loyalty matters most. If your parent criticises your spouse, especially in front of
others, **support your spouse** and speak to your parent privately. Your spouse needs to know you
are on their side. Your parent needs to hear it from you.

## Keeping the warmth

Boundaries work best alongside real effort to stay close:

- Call regularly, and visit often, at times that work for you both.
- Include parents in celebrations and milestones.
- Ask for their stories and advice on things where you genuinely value it.
- Show appreciation. A small gesture goes a long way.

## When to get help

If conflict with in-laws is putting real strain on your marriage, a couples counsellor or a
respected, neutral family elder can help. Seeking help early is a sign of commitment, not failure.

If a family member's behaviour is controlling, threatening or abusive, that is not a boundary
issue. Contact local support services, or in an emergency call 911 (Canada/US), 999 (UK) or
000 (Australia).

Healthy boundaries let everyone keep loving each other, at a distance that works.
