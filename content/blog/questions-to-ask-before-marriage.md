Most couples talk about the easy things before marriage: favourite food, travel plans, how many
cousins will be at the wedding. The questions that shape the next thirty years often go unasked,
because they feel awkward, or because both people assume the answer is obvious.

It rarely is. Two people from similar backgrounds can still have very different ideas about money,
in-laws, children and where "home" will be. Asking early is not a lack of trust. It is respect for
the decision you are both about to make.

Here are the questions to ask before marriage that matter most, grouped by topic. Think of them as premarital questions you ask your partner, not a test. You do not need to cover them all in
one evening. Spread them over several conversations, and listen more than you answer.

## Money

1. How do you handle money today: saver, spender, or somewhere in between?
2. Do you have debt, such as student loans, a car loan or credit cards? How much?
3. Do you support your parents or siblings financially? Do you expect that to continue?
4. Will we combine our money, keep it separate, or do a bit of both?
5. Who will make big money decisions, and how will we decide together?

Money is a common source of stress in marriage. Supporting family is normal in
many cultures, and it is not a red flag. Not mentioning it is. Our guide to [talking about money before marriage](/blog/money-talk-before-marriage) goes deeper.

## Family and in-laws

6. How involved do you expect your parents to be in our daily life?
7. Do you expect us to live with, or near, either set of parents?
8. How do you handle it when your family disagrees with a decision you made?
9. What do you love about your family, and what would you do differently?
10. How should we handle holidays, festivals and visits between two families?

The goal is not to choose a side. It is to understand what "close family" means to each of you
before you are living inside the answer. See also [setting boundaries with in-laws](/blog/setting-boundaries-with-in-laws).

## Children

11. Do you want children? If so, how many, and roughly when?
12. What if we have difficulty having children? Would you consider adoption or treatment?
13. How were you disciplined as a child, and what would you keep or change?
14. Which languages, traditions and values do you want to pass on?
15. If one of us already has children, what role do you see yourself playing in their life?

## Faith and values

16. How important is faith in your daily life, and how do you practise it?
17. How do you expect our children to be raised in terms of faith?
18. What does a good life look like to you in ten years?
19. Which values are not negotiable for you?
20. How do you feel about drinking, smoking, or other lifestyle choices?

## Work, home and where you will live

21. Where do you see us living: this city, another country, back home one day?
22. Would you relocate for my career, or expect me to relocate for yours?
23. How do you feel about one partner staying home, full time or for a while?
24. How will we share cooking, cleaning and childcare?
25. How much time alone do you need, and how much time together?

For couples in the diaspora, question 21 is often the hardest. One partner may dream of going
"back home" while the other has built a life abroad. It is far easier to discuss now than after
the wedding.

## Conflict, health and the hard things

26. When you are upset, do you need space or do you want to talk it out right away?
27. How did your parents argue, and what did that teach you?
28. Is there anything about your health, physical or mental, that I should know?
29. Have you been married or engaged before? What did you learn from it?
30. What would make you feel truly respected in a marriage?

## How to ask without it feeling like an interview

- **Ask one or two at a time.** A walk or a long drive is easier than across a table.
- **Answer the question yourself first.** It shows you are not testing them.
- **Look for honesty, not perfect answers.** "I'm not sure yet, but here's how I'm thinking about
  it" is a good answer.
- **Notice how disagreements feel.** You will not agree on everything. What matters is whether you
  can disagree calmly and still respect each other.
- **Involve family where it helps.** In many families, parents meet early. Some of these questions
  are easier to raise with both families present; others are for the two of you alone. If raising marriage with your own parents is the hard part, start with [talking to Bangladeshi parents about marriage](/blog/talking-to-bangladeshi-parents-about-marriage).

## When the answers don't match

Different answers are not automatically a deal-breaker. Two people can want different numbers of
children and find a middle path. But some gaps are hard to bridge: one person wants children and
the other does not, or one expects to live with their parents for life and the other cannot. If a
gap touches something you called non-negotiable, it is kinder to both of you to take it seriously
now.

A good match is not someone who agrees with you on everything. It is someone whose answers you can
build a life around.
