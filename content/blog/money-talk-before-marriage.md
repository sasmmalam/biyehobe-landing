Money is one of the hardest things to talk about before marriage, and one of the most important.
Couples who never discuss it often discover, a year in, that they had completely different
expectations about spending, saving and supporting family.

The good news: a few honest conversations can prevent many of those surprises. This is a general guide,
not financial advice. For tax, investment or legal questions, speak to a qualified professional.

## Why it feels awkward, and why that's fine

Asking about debt or income can feel like you are judging someone. You are not. You are planning
a shared life. A useful way to start: share your own situation first, then ask.

## What to cover

### 1. Where each of you stands today

- Income (roughly, if exact numbers feel too personal at first)
- Savings
- Debt: student loans, car loans, credit cards, family loans
- Credit history, if either of you has had problems

### 2. Supporting family

For many families, especially immigrant families, supporting parents or siblings is part of life.
Some people send money home every month. Some are paying for a sibling's education, or a parent's
medical care, or helping build a house back home.

This is not a red flag. It is a value many people are proud of. What matters is that both partners
know about it and agree on how it fits into the household budget.

Questions to ask:

- Do you send money to family now? How much, and how often?
- Do you expect that to grow, for example as parents get older?
- How would we decide together if a family member asked for help?

### 3. Joint, separate, or both?

There is no single right answer. Common approaches:

- **Fully joint:** all income goes into shared accounts.
- **Separate with a shared account:** each keeps their own, and both contribute to a joint
  account for rent, bills and shared goals.
- **Proportional:** each contributes a share based on income.

What matters is that you choose together, and revisit it when life changes.

### 4. Big goals

- Buying a home, and when
- Children, and how childcare will be paid for
- Studies or career changes
- Travel, including regular trips to visit family abroad
- Retirement, and where you want to retire

### 5. Spending styles

One of you may love planning every penny; the other may prefer flexibility. Neither is wrong. Talk
about how you will make decisions on larger purchases. Many couples agree on an amount above which
they check with each other first.

### 6. The wedding itself

Weddings, especially multi-day ones across two countries, can be expensive. Agree on a budget
early, and on who is paying for what. Starting a marriage in debt from the wedding puts pressure
on everything else.

## A simple plan for the conversation

1. **Pick a calm moment,** not during an argument or a family visit.
2. **Share your own picture first.**
3. **Go topic by topic,** using the list above.
4. **Write down what you agree**, even informally.
5. **Plan a check-in** a few months after the wedding to see how it is working.

## Red flags to take seriously

- Hiding debt, income, or regular payments to someone
- Refusing to discuss money at all
- Pressure to put assets or loans in one person's name without a clear reason
- Asking you for money early in the relationship, before marriage
- Demands for dowry or expensive "gifts" from either family. In Bangladesh, demanding or giving
  dowry is a criminal offence.

The last one is especially important when meeting people online. A sincere partner will not ask
you to send money, especially someone you have never met in person.

## Money is about values

Behind every money conversation is a question about values: security, generosity, family, freedom.
When you understand why your partner treats money the way they do, the numbers get much easier to
agree on.
